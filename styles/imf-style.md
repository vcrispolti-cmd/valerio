# IMF-Style

Style guide for drafting in the voice of IMF staff documents: Article IV staff reports, policy notes, staff discussion notes (SDNs), WEO/GFSR chapters, IEO evaluations, and concluding statements.

**Trigger:** the user asks to "draft with the IMF-style" (or "IMF style", "in IMF style").

**Sources.** Valerio Crispolti's operating manual *Outstanding IMF-Style Drafting* (revised October 2026, incorporating the 2026 Review of Program Design and Conditionality), plus five reference texts:

- WEO October 2025, Executive Summary
- GFSR April 2023, Chapter 1
- SDN/2023/001, *Geoeconomic Fragmentation and the Future of Multilateralism*
- United States 2026 Article IV, Staff Report (IMF Country Report No. 26/76)
- IEO, *The IMF and Fragile States* (2018)

Mechanical and editorial rules come from the *IMF Editorial Style Guide*, 2nd edition (2024). They are condensed in `styles/imf-style-editorial.md`.

Where institutional guidance and an exemplar differ, current Fund guidance prevails.

---

## 0. How to use this guide when drafting

1. **Identify the product.** Possible products are an Article IV staff report or staff appraisal, a policy note, an SDN or selected issues paper, a WEO/GFSR-type outlook, an IEO-type evaluation, a concluding statement, a program document, or a short note or email. Apply the product rules in §10.
2. **Never fabricate.** Do not invent data, dates, estimates, authorities' positions, or citations. Where a number or fact is needed but not provided, insert a bracketed placeholder such as `[X] percent of GDP` or `[authorities' view to be confirmed]` and flag it.
3. **Draft the governing argument first.** Write one sentence stating the central issue and staff's judgment, then build the architecture (§5) around it.
4. **Apply the editorial rules** in `styles/imf-style-editorial.md` (IMF Editorial Style Guide, 2nd edition). Decide first whether the product is a **publication** or a **staff report / policy note**, because a few conventions differ.
5. **Run the checklist (§12)** before handing back the draft, including the 30-second test.
6. When revising the user's own text, **preserve the policy position and legal meaning**. Flag any change that could alter substance instead of making it silently.

---

## 1. The governing test

> Can a senior reader reconstruct **what changed, why it matters, what staff judges, what should be done, why those actions are prioritized, and how implementation should adapt if assumptions fail**?

Outstanding IMF drafting is disciplined economic judgment made visible. It:

- separates evidence from inference
- calibrates how strong each conclusion is
- explains causal mechanisms
- keeps an institutionally neutral voice
- turns diagnosis into prioritized, feasible, risk-aware advice

**Central discipline.** Concentrate explicit recommendations so they are easy to find, but keep their evidentiary and analytical foundations visible throughout. By the time the reader reaches the Policy Lines, Mission Tasks, or Staff Appraisal, the advice should feel demonstrated rather than newly announced.

**2026 ROC lens for program work.** Show realism, country tailoring, a coherent policy mix, a risk-informed baseline, contingency planning, implementation capacity, fewer but deeper reforms, credible financing, early course correction, and a path to durable external stability.

## 2. The IMF voice

The voice combines **authority with restraint**. It is:

- candid without advocacy
- constructive without diplomatic formula
- evenhanded
- respectful of ownership
- explicit about trade-offs and uncertainty

| Principle | Rule |
|---|---|
| Analytical authority | State the staff assessment clearly; do not overstate what the evidence establishes. |
| Candor | Name policy gaps, risks, and implementation shortfalls directly. |
| Calibration | Match verbs and qualifiers to the strength of the evidence. |
| Constructiveness | Pair criticism with a feasible route to improvement. |
| Evenhandedness | Apply the same evidentiary standard to achievements and shortcomings. |
| Attribution | Distinguish observed facts, staff estimates, authorities' views, projections, and external evidence. |
| Ownership | Show how advice supports agreed objectives; respect legitimate policy choices. |
| Neutrality | No accusatory, partisan, or needlessly political phrasing. |
| Trade-offs | State costs, distributional effects, risks, and second-best considerations. |
| No surprises | Major positions must have been developed in the discussion; characterize authorities' views accurately. |

### Voice calibration

| Avoid | Prefer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "The authorities failed to implement the reform." | "Implementation of the reform fell short of the agreed timetable." | Describes verifiable performance instead of assigning blame. |
| "The policy is wrong." | "The current policy mix is unlikely to achieve the stated objective." | Gives an assessable relationship between instruments and objectives. |
| "The government refuses to…" | "The authorities have not agreed to…" | States a documented position, not an inferred motive. |
| "This will cause…" | "This is expected to…" / "This could…" | Calibrates certainty to the evidence. |
| "There is no evidence…" | "Available evidence does not indicate…" | Limits the claim to the evidence reviewed. |
| "Clearly…" | (state the evidence and conclusion) | Lets analysis, not rhetoric, carry the claim. |

This is **not a synonym table**. Each time, check that the wording reflects the evidence, the confidence level, the institutional position, and the authorities' documented views.

### How candor sounds in the samples

Candor in the samples is firm but never accusatory:

- *"The recent fiscal legislation also represents a missed opportunity to raise revenues while increasing the progressivity of the tax system."* (US Article IV)
- *"Despite this overall positive assessment, the IMF's approach to fragile member states seems conflicted and its impact falls short of what could be achieved."* (IEO)
- *"…initiatives have not been sufficiently bold or adequately sustained, leaving questions about the credibility of the Fund's commitment in this area."* (IEO)
- *"This apparent resilience, however, seems to be largely attributable to temporary factors… rather than to fundamental strength."* (WEO)

The IEO pattern is useful to copy: **recognize contributions first** ("The evaluation recognizes the important contributions…"), then **acknowledge constraints** ("…it must be recognized that work on fragile states is inherently challenging…"), then **deliver the critical judgment** ("Despite this overall positive assessment…").

## 3. Judgment, evidence, and causality

### Layers of judgment

Keep these layers distinguishable. A lead judgment is the compressed endpoint of the chain, not an unsupported opinion.

| Layer | Example |
|---|---|
| Fact | Inflation declined from 23 to 15 percent. |
| Interpretation | The decline largely reflected lower food inflation and tighter monetary conditions. |
| Assessment | Disinflation appears broadly consistent with the authorities' objective, although underlying pressures remain elevated. |
| Risk judgment | Risks to the disinflation path remain tilted upward. |
| Policy judgment | Further easing should remain gradual and data dependent. |

A confident sentence is not necessarily a strong judgment. If the evidentiary bridge is missing, **strengthen or qualify the reasoning**; do not just soften or intensify the wording.

### Evidence tiers and matching language

| Tier | Examples | Language |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Direct evidence | Official data, audited statements, laws, program targets, observed outcomes | "Data show…", "The law provides…", "The target was met…" |
| 2. Strong analytical evidence | Models, econometrics, DSA, ESA, stress tests, established literature | "Model estimates suggest…", "The DSA indicates…", "Staff estimates…" |
| 3. Inference | Staff interpretation of several facts, indirect evidence, cross-country comparison | "The deterioration appears to reflect…", "…seems to be largely attributable to…" |
| 4. Hypothesis | Plausible but unestablished mechanism or scenario | "One possible explanation is…", "If sustained, this could…" |

**Evidence tests:**

- **Traceability:** the reader can see the source or basis.
- **Relevance:** the evidence establishes the proposition, not just relates to the topic.
- **Sufficiency:** the conclusion is no stronger than the evidence permits.
- **Consistency:** text, tables, and figures tell the same story.
- **Comparability:** periods, definitions, and denominators are aligned.
- **Alternative explanations:** the most material competing explanation has been considered.
- **Proportionality:** uncertainty is calibrated to how material the decision is.

### Causal chains

The causal chain is the central engine of the analysis:

> shock or policy → behavioral response → transmission mechanism → macroeconomic effect → distributional, financial, or external consequence → risk → policy implication

*Example (manual):* "Electricity tariffs below cost recovery weaken utility cash generation; payment arrears then accumulate across the energy value chain; contingent or quasi-fiscal obligations rise; fiscal risks increase and space for priority spending declines; tariff policy, collection performance, loss reduction, and arrears management therefore need to be addressed as a coherent package."

*Example (WEO):* tariffs are "supply shocks for tariff-imposing countries and demand shocks for the targeted, with uncertainty being a negative demand shock all around."

*Example (US Art. IV):* "…the higher limits for interest deductibility convey an overly generous benefit (effectively subsidizing debt-financed capital spending) and continues to make the effective corporate tax rate dependent on the firm's capital structure (further incentivizing corporate leverage)." Note how the parentheses carry the mechanism.

Check each chain:

- Is the initial shock precisely identified, and is the behavioral response plausible?
- Is the transmission channel explicit, and is the time horizon clear?
- Are macroeconomic and distributional consequences distinguished?
- Are feedback loops or offsetting effects addressed?
- Does the proposed instrument target the identified mechanism?

## 4. Calibrated vocabulary (from the samples)

**Hedging and calibration:**

- "appears increasingly likely", "seems to be largely attributable to", "is expected to", "is projected to", "could", "may"
- "broadly", "somewhat", "moderately", "on balance", "so far"

**Risk language:**

- "Risks to the outlook remain tilted to the downside."
- "Risks … are balanced while risks to inflation are to the upside."
- "represents a growing stability tail risk"
- "a potentially important source of vulnerability"
- "raises the specter of"
- "On the upside, …" (always give the upside after the downside list)

**Analytical verbs:**

- reflects, raises, constrains, supports, requires
- amplify, transmit, spill over, erode, undermine, dampen, suppress, stifle

**Advice verbs, in rising order of strength:**

1. "could consider" / "there is scope to"
2. "should"
3. "will need to" / "is needed"
4. "is essential" / "is critical" / "remains a priority"
5. "should act swiftly" / "without any further delay"

Reserve the strongest forms for macro-critical, time-sensitive items.

**Endorsing language:**

- "it was appropriate for the Fed to…" (quoted from a staff report; in publications write "Federal Reserve")
- "Recent improvements in … are welcome. However, further actions will be needed…"
- "…has benefited the economy and should be maintained."

**Continuity with prior advice:**

- "In line with staff's long-standing policy advice, …"
- "As argued in previous Article IV consultations, …"
- "As outlined in the 2024 Article IV consultation, …"

**Preferred alternatives** (see the US Article IV): "A preferred approach would be to…", followed by the specific instrument and a parenthetical reason it works ("a proven policy that incentivizes work").

## 5. Document and paragraph architecture

### Document sequence

1. **Context:** the policy problem and why it matters now.
2. **Diagnostics:** drivers, constraints, and deviations.
3. **Outlook:** baseline and main assumptions.
4. **Risks:** triggers, transmission, impact, and responses.
5. **Policy discussion:** the current stance compared with objectives and feasible alternatives.
6. **Advice:** prioritized instruments, sequencing, trade-offs, and contingencies.
7. **Synthesis:** integrated staff judgment and formal recommendation.

### The 30-second test

Read only the title, headings, the first sentence of each paragraph, figure and table titles, and the appraisal. A senior reader should be able to reconstruct the whole argument. If they cannot, the problem is architectural, not stylistic.

### Bold lead sentences

In staff reports, each numbered paragraph opens with a **bold lead sentence that is the paragraph's judgment**. The body then supplies evidence and mechanism. Examples from the US report:

> **63. The U.S. economy has performed well in 2025, quickly adapting to a significant shift in policies…**
>
> **65. Persistently high fiscal deficits, the upward path for the public debt-GDP ratio, and increasing levels of short-term debt-GDP represent a growing stability tail risk to the U.S. and global economy.**
>
> **69. Financial stability risks are moderate but there is scope to strengthen oversight.**

In SDNs, a bold lead sentence states the finding for each block ("**The cost of GEF can be very large, especially for vulnerable countries and populations.**"). In WEO executive summaries, the first sentence of each paragraph is an unbolded topic sentence that carries the judgment ("The rules of the global economy are in flux.", "After a resilient start, the global economy is showing signs of a moderate slowdown, as predicted.").

### Paragraph architectures

These are functional models, not rigid templates.

| Type | Sequence |
|---|---|
| Analytical | Judgment → evidence → mechanism → qualification → implication |
| Policy | Problem → assessment → options → trade-off → staff recommendation |
| Risk | Risk → trigger → transmission → impact → mitigation |
| Program/institutional | Objective → constraint → action → sequencing → monitoring/contingency |
| Authorities' views | Issue → authorities' position → rationale → agreement or difference |
| Appraisal | Progress → remaining problem → integrated judgment → prioritized advice → formal recommendation |

### "At a Glance" and executive-summary bullets

These are GFSR-style. Use full-sentence bullets, each making one judgment with its mechanism, and keep them dense but self-contained:

> "Shifting patterns of deposits across different institutions could raise funding costs for banks, which could restrict their ability to provide credit to the economy."

## 6. Sentence-level prose

- **Make the actor visible.** Use subject–verb–object order so the actor, action, and consequence are clear.
- **One principal claim per sentence.** Split sentences that pack in unrelated propositions or several actions.
- **Avoid nominalization.** Write "revenue declined", not "there was a decline in revenue".
- **Avoid rhetorical intensifiers.** Do not use "clearly", "very important", or "significant" (unless statistical) without evidence behind them.
- **Use concrete references.** Name the mechanism or policy instead of writing "this issue".
- **Make comparisons meaningful.** Always state the baseline, previous forecast, target, or counterfactual. Example: "an improvement relative to the July WEO Update—but cumulatively 0.2 percentage point below forecasts made before the policy shifts in the October 2024 WEO."
- **Use concessive structures** to show balance: "Despite…", "While…, …", "…, although…", "…; however, …". For example: "While there is little doubt that the regulatory changes … have made the financial system generally more resilient, the fundamental question … is whether…"
- **Use em dashes** (no spaces) for appositive examples and asides, e.g. "temporary factors—such as front-loading of trade and investment—".
- **Use parentheses** for mechanisms, examples, and definitions, e.g. "(a proven policy that incentivizes work)".
- **Preserve legal and policy meaning.** Never simplify in a way that changes obligations, conditionality, or recommendations.

## 7. Mechanical conventions

The full mechanical rules are in **`styles/imf-style-editorial.md`**, a condensed version of the *IMF Editorial Style Guide*, 2nd edition (2024). Apply that file to every draft. Its scope table says which rules change for staff reports and policy notes compared with publications.

Rules that come up most often:

**Numbers:**
- Write "percent", never "%" in text.
- "0.5 percentage point" is singular at 1 or less; "1.25 percentage points" is plural above 1.
- Fractions are fine: "about 1½ percent", "3½–4 percent of GDP".
- Spell out numbers below 10, except for percentages, ratios, and money.

**Ranges and dashes:**
- En-dash ranges: "2025–26", "1990–2010".
- Hyphenated ratios: "debt-to-GDP ratio".
- Hyphenate a compound adjective before a noun ("medium-term outlook") but not after it ("over the medium term").

**Dates:**
- In publication text, write "in the fourth quarter of 2025" and "the second half of 2027". Keep "2025:Q4" and "2027:H2" for figures and tables.
- Staff reports tolerate "Q4" and "2027H1".
- Name flagship vintages: "the April 2025 *World Economic Outlook*", "the July 2025 *World Economic Outlook Update*". Italicize the report and keep the database roman.

**Names and references:**
- Write "the IMF" in publications. Staff reports also use "the Fund".
- Write "the IMF staff" with a plural verb in publications, and "staff" without an article in staff reports.
- Write "$100", since the US dollar is assumed. Staff reports commonly use "US$".
- Write "the United States" as a noun; "US" is an adjective only.
- Write "Federal Reserve", not "the Fed". Write "euro area", "advanced economies", "emerging market and developing economies".

**Banned forms:**
- No contractions.
- No "e.g.", "i.e.", "etc.", or "et al."; write "for example", "that is", "and so on", "and others".
- No "and/or", "via", "utilize", or "additionally".
- No "impact" as a general noun or verb (use "effect" or "affect").
- No "due to" except after a verb.
- No "following" when "after" is meant.

**Abbreviations:**
- Define at first use only if the term is used three or more times in that section, and define again in each chapter, box, and figure note.
- Drop ad hoc abbreviations such as "GFC".
- Always write in full "global financial crisis", "sub-Saharan Africa", and "*World Economic Outlook*"; GDP is never spelled out.

**Lists and punctuation:**
- Use the serial comma.
- Number run-in lists (1), (2), (3).
- A vertical list takes a full-sentence lead-in ending in a colon.

**Headings:**
- Headline-style capitalization, and they should state a finding where possible ("A Financial System Tested by Higher Inflation and Interest Rates").
- Publications do not number headings and do not bold paragraph lead sentences.
- Staff reports use numbered paragraphs with bold leads, sections "A.", "B.", and run-in "**Authorities' views.**" paragraphs.

**Figures and tables:**
- Each needs a number, a what–where–when title (plus an optional narrative head stating the takeaway), a unit line, "Source(s):" with "IMF staff calculations" last, then "Note:", and an in-text callout.

**Citations:** Chicago author–date, for example "(Bandara and Yu 2003; IMF 2023)" or "Mahler and others (2021)".

**Closing formula of an Article IV staff appraisal:** "It is recommended that the next Article IV consultation take place on the standard 12-month cycle."

## 8. Policy advice: constraint-aware problem solving

Advice is not a reform wish list. For each recommendation, be able to answer:

- What is the problem and the binding constraint?
- Which instrument directly addresses the mechanism, and what are the alternatives?
- What are the macroeconomic, fiscal, financial, and distributional trade-offs, and who bears the costs?
- What can realistically be implemented with current capacity, and what must happen first?
- What capacity development is needed?
- What could derail implementation, and what trigger activates the contingency?

**Prioritization.** An item is high priority only if it is macro-critical, time-sensitive, instrumental to other reforms, or necessary to protect program objectives. Do not label everything urgent. Prefer **fewer, deeper reforms** (2026 ROC).

**Pattern from the samples** (US Art. IV ¶65):

1. Diagnosis: "Persistently high fiscal deficits … represent a growing stability tail risk"
2. Quantification: "deficit … in the 7–7½ percent of GDP range … debt exceeding 140 percent of GDP by 2031"
3. Continuity: "In line with staff's long-standing policy advice"
4. Calibrated ask: "an adjustment of around 4 percent of GDP relative to the current baseline"
5. Instruments: "both an increase in federal revenues and a rebalancing of entitlement programs"
6. Distributional safeguard: "To protect poorer households … improvements in the social safety net will also be needed"

**Tiered or conditional advice** (SDN ¶V): "In areas of common interest … multilateral effort remains the best approach. … In areas where preferences are not well aligned … plurilateral initiatives could be a practical way forward. … In areas where … countries increasingly resort to unilateral actions, credible 'guardrails' may be needed."

**Conditional monetary advice** (US Art. IV ¶64): "A larger monetary easing would need to be predicated on a material worsening in labor market prospects and an absence of increasing inflationary pressures." This shows a clear trigger.

## 9. Risks, scenarios, and contingency

For each material risk, cover these elements:

| Element | Question |
|---|---|
| Risk | What could cause the baseline to deviate? |
| Trigger | What observable event or threshold would signal it? |
| Transmission | How would it affect the economy or program? |
| Impact | What is the direction, scale, and horizon? |
| Mitigation | What reduces probability or impact beforehand? |
| Contingency | What action follows if the trigger is reached? |
| Monitoring | Which indicators are tracked, and by whom? |

**Realism test.** Baseline assumptions, financing, reform payoffs, multipliers, timing, political economy, and capacity must be mutually consistent. Where uncertainty is high, say which assumptions matter most and how policy adjusts if they fail.

**WEO risk-paragraph pattern:**

1. Overall balance: "Risks to the outlook remain tilted to the downside, as they were in previous WEO reports."
2. One sentence per downside risk, each in the form *shock → channel → consequence*: "Further escalation of protectionist measures, including nontariff barriers, could suppress investment, disrupt supply chains, and stifle productivity growth."
3. Upside risks: "On the upside, a breakthrough in trade negotiations could lower tariffs and reduce uncertainty."

## 10. Product-specific rules

**Article IV staff report / staff appraisal:**

- The body holds candid, substantive policy discussion and **Authorities' views** after each policy section.
- The Staff Appraisal **synthesizes and prioritizes**. It does not introduce new major judgments, it opens each paragraph with a bold judgment, and it ends with the consultation-cycle recommendation.
- Apply macro-criticality to decide coverage, integrate domestic and external stability and spillovers, and keep forecasts realistic.

**Authorities' views paragraphs:**

- Attribute explicitly and accurately, and present the authorities' rationale in its strongest fair form, in their own framing. In the US example, staff report the administration's characterization ("a once-in-a-generation piece of legislation…") without endorsing or rebutting it inside the paragraph.
- Never infer motives. Distinguish agreement on objectives from disagreement on instruments, timing, or calibration.
- Staff's response sits in staff paragraphs, and the Appraisal must not erase a material disagreement.

**Policy note (mission brief):**

- Analytical sections prove the case: context, performance, diagnostics, outlook, risks, assessment, authorities' positions, and constraints.
- Concentrate operational advice, negotiating objectives, conditionality, and contingencies in clearly labelled **Policy Lines / Mission Priorities / Mission Tasks / Program Design** sections, using verbs like "assess, discuss, recommend, seek agreement on, propose".

**Programs and PCIs:**

- Link each measure to program objectives and the BoP problem.
- Show policy-mix coherence, financing realism, ownership, and capacity.
- Prefer fewer, deeper reforms.
- Specify monitoring, course correction, and contingencies.
- Distinguish program compliance from durable resolution of the imbalance.

**FCS, LIC, and low-capacity settings:**

- Do not confuse limited capacity with weak ownership.
- Tailor scope, sequencing, and conditionality to capability, and identify institutional preconditions.
- Integrate CD where it addresses the binding constraint.
- Treat political-economy constraints analytically and neutrally.

**SDN / analytical policy paper:**

- The abstract states the risk, what the note does ("This note explores… It identifies… It explores… Finally, it suggests…"), and the way forward.
- Define the core concept precisely, including what it excludes ("GEF does not include fragmentation arising from autonomous shifts in preferences or technology").
- Signpost the structure ("The rest of the note proceeds as follows…"), mark scope limits in footnotes, and end with "A Way Forward" and open questions.

**WEO/GFSR outlook and executive summary:**

- Open with the big-picture judgment and track forecast revisions against earlier vintages.
- Present baseline numbers, then the balance of risks with downside and upside, then a policy paragraph that opens with the overarching task ("The task ahead is to restore confidence through credible, predictable, and sustainable policy actions.").
- Cross-reference chapters ("As Chapter 3 demonstrates, …").

**IEO-style evaluation:**

- Structure: purpose and scope → recognition of contributions → constraints → "Despite this overall positive assessment…" → concerns, each as a bold run-in heading with a candid paragraph → numbered recommendations.
- Each recommendation takes the form "**Recommendation N: Short title.** [Actor] should [action], including [specifics]."

**Concluding statement / public communication:** shorter sentences, fewer acronyms, the same calibration, and no market-sensitive content.

## 11. Editing and compression method

Use this when revising a draft.

1. Reconstruct the governing argument without editing sentences.
2. Test evidence, judgment, causality, and alternatives.
3. Test document, section, and paragraph architecture.
4. Test advice, prioritization, sequencing, and contingencies.
5. Revise voice, attribution, calibration, and neutrality.
6. Compress repetition, chronology, procedural detail, and generic language.
7. Reconcile text, tables, figures, annexes, policy lines, and appraisal.
8. Final pass: the 30-second test and a no-fabrication consistency check.

## 12. Master checklist (run before returning any draft)

- [ ] **Voice:** candid, calibrated, constructive, neutral, properly attributed.
- [ ] **Judgment:** fact, inference, assessment, risk, and advice are distinguishable.
- [ ] **Evidence:** claims are traceable; wording matches evidentiary strength.
- [ ] **Causality:** mechanisms and transmission channels are explicit.
- [ ] **Architecture:** one governing narrative; leads alone reconstruct the argument.
- [ ] **Paragraphs:** each performs a defined function.
- [ ] **Advice:** addresses the binding constraint with feasible instruments.
- [ ] **Prioritization:** critical actions are separated from desirable ones.
- [ ] **Realism:** assumptions, financing, capacity, and timing are consistent.
- [ ] **Risk:** triggers, impacts, mitigation, and contingencies are specified.
- [ ] **Views:** authorities' positions are accurate and separate from staff's.
- [ ] **Visuals and headings:** they state findings and advance the argument.
- [ ] **Sequencing:** analysis demonstrates the case; advice is concentrated and identifiable.
- [ ] **Appraisal:** integrates, prioritizes, and makes the formal recommendation, with no new major judgments.
- [ ] **Compression:** no repetition, data dumping, empty qualifiers, or generic catalogs.
- [ ] **Integrity:** no unsupported facts, invented causality, or altered policy stance. Placeholders are flagged.
- [ ] **House style:** the copyediting pass in `styles/imf-style-editorial.md` §14 is done, using the right column of its scope table for staff reports and policy notes.

**Senior-review questions:**

- Can the central issue be stated in one sentence?
- Does each section change the reader's understanding or decision?
- Would a skeptical reviewer understand why staff prefers this option?
- Has any rewrite altered the policy position?
